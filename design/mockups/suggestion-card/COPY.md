# Suggestion card: copy for every state

- **Written by:** the CMO seat (an AI advisor role), for the UIUX seat's three mockup directions (`BRIEF.md`).
- **Date:** 2026-11-05
- **Status:** proposal copy. **The team locks the words.** Run it past `/icp` before locking. The CTO checks every technical promise marked `[CTO]`. The CSO checks every safety or study line marked `[CSO]`.
- **All numbers below are sample data.** The counts (4 of 6 and so on) are made up to show the shape of the line, not results.

## How to read this

- **Three voices.** **A, calm friend:** warm and plain. **B, plain instrument:** neutral and precise, almost no personality. **C, quiet minimal:** the fewest words that still carry the reason and how sure we are.
- The voices are independent of the three visual directions. A natural pairing is voice A with "Clear Card", voice B with "Forecast" and voice C with "Quiet", but any voice works in any direction.
- **The sample clock** reads 3:20 pm when the card arrives. Times are 12-hour with "am/pm", because the users are in the US.
- **The button rule:** the suggested action and **Keep going** are the same size and weight, side by side. "Primary" below means the suggested action, not a bigger button. An easy dismiss had the strongest effect in one sec's study (PNAS 2023, in `09-design/studies/design-studies.md`).
- **Where "Did it help?" comes from.** "A walk helped" means the student tapped **It helped** after an earlier walk (state 7), or that their practice score went up afterwards. We don't claim breaks make anyone smarter: the evidence that breaks improve demanding mental work is null (`12-research/science-and-tech-evidence.md`, as summarised in `hot.md`). `[CTO][CSO]` lock this definition before the counts go on a slide.
- **The confidence words** form a fixed ladder: **Not sure · Fairly sure · Quite sure**. We never say "Certain" or show a percentage (Gigerenzer 2005, in `09-design/design-evidence.md` §3). `[CTO]` sets the thresholds.

## Name / wordmark options (for the top of the card, not final branding)

| Name | Why it fits | Concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Halftime** | Everyone in the US knows it means "pause, then go back in". It covers students and athletes without saying "athlete", and it's calm, not hype. | `[check]`: "Halftime" is used by other products and media (for example sports shows and the Super Bowl halftime show branding). Search USPTO class 9/42. |
| **Pace** | Short, neutral, and about the session, not the body. | `[check]`: crowded; many fitness and running apps use "Pace". Weak to own. |
| **Breather** | Plain, friendly, says "short break". | `[check]`: Breather was a US room-booking brand. It also leans entirely toward breaks, against the "keep going is a real answer" rule. |
| **Seam** | It names the moment it arrives: the gap between tasks. Quiet and unusual. | Needs a one-line explanation, so it fails the 5-second test without the product line. `[check]` for app and tech marks. |
| **Steady** | Matches the "keep going" answer as much as the break. | `[check]`: used by fintech and wellness apps (e.g. the Steady income app). |

**My pick: Halftime** (inference). It's the only option where the name itself says the product's promise ("stop briefly, then go back in"), and it doesn't lean toward breaks over keeping going. Do a trademark search before anything public.

## One-line pitch options (proposal slide)

1. **Safe:** "Should you keep studying or take a walk? It answers for right now, and shows why and how sure it is."
2. **Sharp:** "The study break app that tells you when it doesn't know."
3. **Smallest audience:** "For Columbia students in long study sessions: one suggestion at a time, with its reasons, using your watch and laptop but never what you type." `[CTO]` checks "never what you type".

**My pick: #1.** It passes the 5-second test: what it does (keep going or walk), when (right now), and what's different (why and how sure). It also makes no outcome claim, which the evidence doesn't support (breaks reduce fatigue; gains on demanding work are unproven). Say #2 out loud in the pitch as the memorable line, after the slide.

## Recommendation: voice A (calm friend), held to C's length on the card face

**Pick A.** Use one voice everywhere. Two rules keep it honest: the card face stays under about 30 words, and the sources sheet borrows B's exact record labels, because records should read like records.

Why:
1. **It's the brief.** `design/PRODUCT.md` sets the tone: "Calm, plain, kind, honest. A thoughtful friend who is good with data, not a coach or a doctor." A is that voice. B drops the "kind", and C drops "plain" whenever a fragment needs decoding.
2. **The words aren't what persuades, so they can afford to be kind.** In one sec's study (PNAS 2023), the easy dismiss had the strongest effect and the persuasive message alone had none (`09-design/studies/design-studies.md`). Warmth in A isn't there to push anyone toward the break. It makes saying no feel normal. The equal-weight "Keep going" button does the real work in every voice.
3. **Serious moments need warmth.** `/polish` (clarify) says consent, deletion and escalation should be "warm and plain, never cute". B's escalation card ("Talking to someone is an option.") reads like a form, and C's ("Want to talk to someone?") is a bare question at the worst moment. A is the only voice that works for state 9.
4. **C's fragments cost clarity.** `/polish` asks for complete sentences that translate, with no fragments joined together. "Fairly sure · helped 4 of 6 like this" makes a tired reader work out what "like this" means. A's "4 of your last 6 afternoons like this" names the reference class, which is the point of a word plus a count (Gigerenzer 2005, `09-design/design-evidence.md` §3).
5. **The risk in B is tone, not facts.** Athletes rejected AI summaries that judged their workouts "in one fixed tone" (a 2026 study of r/Strava threads, in `09-design/studies/design-studies.md`). That's sourced for athletes, and we're inferring that it carries over to students. B is safest for accuracy, but it reads most like a grade.

Where C still wins: if the team picks the "Quiet" direction, use **C's headlines** ("Keep going", "5-min walk") with **A's reason and confidence lines** under them. That's the only mix I'd accept.

**What to test before locking** (an inference, not evidence):
- Run all three voices through `/icp`, watching Sam (1 am) and Jordan (no watch).
- In the pilot, measure two things per voice: the share of cards answered within 10 s (either button), and how often students tap "This looks wrong".
- Don't judge the voices by how often students choose the break. A voice that gets more breaks may just be pushier.

## Words we never use

**Hype:** unlock, supercharge, optimise/optimize, boost, seamless, elevate, empower, effortless, cutting-edge, revolutionary, game-changer, smarter, peak, AI-powered (as a benefit), productivity hack.

**Clinical or diagnostic:** fatigue, abnormal, risk, readiness score, brain, cognitive load, diagnose, symptom, disorder, insomnia, sleep debt, deprived, clinical, medical-grade, detected, warning, alert.

**Feelings we can't measure** (we never say how they feel): tired, exhausted, stressed, burned out, anxious, overwhelmed, you seem, you look, you feel, restless, distracted.

**Grading sleep or the body:** bad/poor/good sleep, only (as in "only 5 hours"), not enough sleep, behind, score, red/green, out of range.

**Guilt and pressure:** streak, missed, skipped, late, failed, broke, don't forget, you should have, are you sure?, last chance, you need to, must.

**Banned buttons:** OK, Submit, Learn more, Get started, Got it, Continue (alone, without an object), Close (alone, where "Back to suggestion" fits).

**Marks and style:** exclamation marks, emoji, percentages for confidence ("72% sure"), "Certain".

**Promises we can't keep yet** (only after the CTO confirms each one): "we'll notify you", "data never leaves your device", "Data Not Collected".
