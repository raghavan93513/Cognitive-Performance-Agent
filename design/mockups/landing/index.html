How it works, in four steps.

Scroll through one afternoon. Sample data.

  1. 1

    It notices.

    A small app on your laptop counts how long you've studied and how often you switch apps.

  2. 2

    It reads your watch, carefully.

    Sleep, heart rate, resting heart rate and HRV, each compared with your own usual, never anyone else’s. Plain code does the math, not an AI. No watch? It skips this step.

  3. 3

    It suggests, and shows why.

    A small card opens: keep going, a 5-minute walk, or a longer break. Each reason shows where it came from and how sure it is.

  4. 4

    You decide.

    Walk, keep going or snooze. It asks once, then stays quiet.

Your watch knows your body. Your study apps know your hours. Halftime connects them to your day.

Wearables like WHOOP and Fitbit track sleep and heart data. Productivity apps time your work. Halftime is the layer in between: it reads both, checks your calendar, and suggests a break only when one fits.

What it reads

  • How long you've been studying, and your breaks
  • How often you switch apps, and which kind (like notes, reading, video or chat), not the app's name
  • Sleep, heart rate and HRV, if you connect a watch or ring
  • Your next class, if you turn on your calendar

What it never reads

  • What you type, read or browse
  • Your messages or your screen
  • Your location
  • Your watch's own scores, like Readiness or Recovery

When it doesn't know, it says so.

For the first week it's still learning what's normal for you, and it tells you that instead of guessing.

Still learning your usual sleep · 4 of 7 nights

Some things need a person.

If your sleep has been short for weeks, it offers someone to talk to, in the morning, never mid-study. It never tells your coach, team or school.

Halftime is a wellness tool, not medical advice.
